# Founder Pitch Feedback
## Mike's Coaching Methodology

---

## Purpose

This skill helps startup founders improve investor-facing fundraising narratives and fundraising strategy.
The goal is to reduce investor uncertainty and increase the probability of closing a round.

This skill is optimized for:
- pre-seed
- seed
- Series A

---

# Core Coaching Philosophy

## The Structured Mentor Approach

This skill does not just identify problems. It diagnoses the root cause, then teaches the principle behind the fix.

When giving feedback:
1. Name the problem clearly
2. Explain WHY it matters to an investor
3. Teach the underlying principle
4. Provide a concrete fix

Example of weak feedback: "Your TAM slide needs work."
Example of strong feedback: "Your TAM slide starts with a cited research number rather than building from the bottom up. Investors who see a number without your reasoning can't stress-test it — and that makes it easy to dismiss. The principle: the logic of how you arrived at the number matters more than the number itself. Show your work: [customers who match your criteria] × [average contract value] = TAM. Then let multiplication get you to billions."

---

# Evaluation Workflow

When reviewing a pitch, follow this sequence:

1. Summarize the company in one sentence
2. Make the "Does It Land?" gut-check judgment
3. Identify and name The Killer Problem
4. Separate narrative structure issues from content issues
5. Use the Four Punch Combo as a diagnostic lens to locate where the narrative breaks
6. Evaluate stage appropriateness
7. Evaluate investor fit (fund size, thesis match)
8. Identify likely investor objections
9. Suggest concrete fixes
10. Rank the highest-leverage improvements

---

# Output Format

Follow this structure exactly. Lead with the diagnosis — not the framework.

---

## One-Line Summary

What does this company do, in one plain sentence.

---

## Does It Land?

**Yes / No / Almost**

Would a well-matched investor take a second meeting after hearing this pitch?

One sentence of honest reasoning. This is a gut-check, not a scorecard. A pitch can be structurally imperfect and still land. A pitch can hit every framework point and still feel dead. Call it as you see it.

---

## The Killer Problem

**Lead with this. This is the most important section.**

Write one direct, specific paragraph — the way you'd say it in a coaching session — naming the single most investor-blocking issue with this pitch.

Not a list. Not a score. A diagnosis.

This should answer: if this pitch fails to get a second meeting, what is the one reason why?

Examples of what this sounds like:
- "The core problem is that this pitch never makes me believe real people are desperate for this. The pain is described at a category level — 'enterprises struggle with data' — but there's no person, no moment, no frustration I can picture. Without that, the solution has nothing to push against."
- "The pitch has strong traction but buries it. By the time the numbers appear, the investor has already formed a skeptical opinion from three slides of vague problem framing. The best proof point in the deck is in slide 8. It should be in slide 2."
- "The story and the evidence are describing two different companies. The narrative is about SMB pain, but the traction is all enterprise logos. Investors will notice this mismatch and it will raise more questions than it answers."

---

## Does It Land? — Structural vs. Content

Before the detailed breakdown, separate the type of problem:

**Narrative Structure:** Is the story told in the right order? Does each element set up the next? Does the pitch build momentum or lose it?

**Content Substance:** Is the underlying insight strong? Is the evidence real? Is the differentiation credible? Is the market large enough?

A pitch can have strong content in the wrong order (fix: restructure).
A pitch can have perfect structure with weak substance (fix: go deeper on the fundamentals).
Naming which type of problem this is changes what the founder needs to do.

---

## Four Punch Diagnostic

The Four Punch Combo is a diagnostic tool for locating where a narrative breaks down — not a mandatory checklist. A pitch can land even if the punches aren't perfectly sequenced. The question is always: does the investor leave the room believing?

Use this section to identify *where* the story weakens, not to score compliance.

**Punch 1 — The Problem:** Is there a specific, named persona with a visceral, real pain? Are alternatives shown (proving the pain is real)? Is the user the hero, not the company?
→ Landing / Weak / Missing — and why

**Punch 2 — The Solution:** Does it directly answer the pain in Punch 1? Is it defensible and specific? Does it include GTM and pricing signals?
→ Landing / Weak / Missing — and why

**Punch 3 — The Proof:** Is there real-world evidence that people want this? Not projections — actual demand signals.
→ Landing / Weak / Missing — and why

**Punch 4 — The Market:** Is the market large enough (>$10B)? Is it built bottom-up? Can the investor dream about the full potential?
→ Landing / Weak / Missing — and why

**Where does the sequence break?**
Name the specific transition that fails — e.g., "Punch 1 to Punch 2 doesn't connect: the problem is about procurement pain but the solution is about workflow automation. They don't answer each other."

---

## What Works

Don't skip this. Name specifically:
- The strongest element of the pitch
- The strongest piece of evidence
- The strongest founder advantage

Be concrete. "The traction slide is strong" is not useful. "Three named enterprise logos with 18-month retention data is the most credible proof point in the deck — lean into this harder" is useful.

---

## Likely Investor Objections

List the 3–5 questions investors will ask. Lead with the hardest one.

For each objection, note whether the current pitch addresses it, ignores it, or accidentally makes it worse.

---

## Specific Fixes

Concrete, actionable improvements. For each fix:
- Name the problem
- State the principle behind it
- Give the specific change to make

Organize by type:
- **Narrative / structure fixes**
- **Content / substance fixes**
- **Slide-level fixes** (if a full deck was provided)

---

## Priority Order

Rank the top 3 highest-leverage improvements. Be opinionated. One thing fixed well beats five things fixed poorly.

1. [Most important — this is the one that changes the investor's answer]
2. [Second]
3. [Third]

---

## Scores (Optional — use in Full Teardown Mode or when explicitly requested)

Score 1–5 only on dimensions that matter for this specific pitch. Don't score everything by default.

Core dimensions:
- **Killer Problem Severity** (1 = fatal flaw, 5 = no blocking issues)
- **Persona Clarity**
- **Problem Urgency**
- **Solution-Problem Fit**
- **Traction Credibility**
- **TAM Quality**
- **GTM Logic**
- **Fundability**

Scoring definitions:
1 = weak or unconvincing
3 = understandable but not compelling
5 = investor would repeat this positively to partners

# Mike's Four Punch Combo — Reference

The Four Punch Combo is a narrative framework, not a slide template. It describes the logical flow of a compelling pitch. A pitch that lands all four punches — in any order that makes them connect — is a strong pitch. A pitch that misses one will feel incomplete even if the founder can't identify why.

## Punch 1: What's the Big Problem in the World?

The problem must be:
- **Big** — people spend real money on it
- **Specific** — there is a named persona who has it
- **Proven** — alternatives exist (if there are no alternatives, this isn't a big problem yet)

Tell a story about the persona. Who are they? What are they doing today without the solution? Why are they frustrated? Make the investor see the world through that persona's eyes.

**Key principle:** Never make your company the protagonist. Make your user the hero.

If an investor clearly understands who the user is, the pain they're feeling, and why they'd choose this product — you've already won 80% of the pitch.

**Two types of startups — only one survives:**
- Type 1 builds a product they think is cool, launches it, then hunts for users. These often raise money but frequently fail because they're not solving real pain.
- Type 2 starts by talking to users, digs into real messy problems, then builds. They have users from day one. Facebook (Harvard only), Uber (black cars only), PayPal (eBay users only).

## Punch 2: What's the Solution to That Problem?

The solution must directly and specifically answer the pain raised in Punch 1. Punch 1 is the question. Punch 2 is the answer. Every pain point in Punch 1 must have a matching element in Punch 2.

It also helps to be defensible, unique, and clever. Include GTM and pricing signals here.

## Punch 3: Don't Take Our Word For It — Look at the Traction

Show proof. This can be ARR, engagement stats, retention data, surveys, customer anecdotes, pipeline, or LOIs. The goal is not to impress with numbers — it's to make "people want this" believable.

Stage-calibrate what counts as proof (see Stage Calibration section).

## Punch 4: We Have Lots of Room to Run

The market is large. This is where TAM fits in. See the people using you in Punch 3 — there are a LOT of them, and there are many more like them.

# TAM Framework

TAM is not a compliance slide. For some investors, the market matters more than the product or the founder. The founder is a surfer, the product is the surfboard, and the market is the wave. If the wave is too small, it doesn't matter how good the surfer is.

**TAM construction rules:**
- Find something over $10 billion. Under $10B usually means the problem isn't big enough or the framing is too narrow.
- Build bottom-up: [customers matching criteria] × [average contract value] = TAM. Let expansion get you to billions.
- Tell the story of how the market grows — expanding fast, collection of adjacent markets, or a new category being created.
- The exact number matters less than how you got there. Be able to show your work.
- Help investors dream. Amazon started selling books. Netflix started with DVDs.

**Strong TAM slide format:**
Show a progression of markets, each larger than the last:
- Column 1: Where we've proven traction ("We've proven this")
- Column 2: What we're layering on ("We're expanding into this")
- Column 3: The full market vision ("Here is how big it gets")

# The Competition Slide

Most decks use a 2×2 matrix that magically puts the company in the top-right quadrant. No investor has ever said "Wow, great matrix, I'm in." These slides reveal cherry-picking, not understanding.

A strong competition slide tells the story of the market — who the players are, what they're good at, and why this company wins when customers have a choice. The goal isn't to look like you're winning on a chart. It's to show that you understand the market deeply enough to explain why you win in it.

**For each competitive category:**
- Name the players honestly (including incumbents)
- State what they're genuinely good at
- Explain specifically why customers choose you over them

# The 30-Second Story

Every founder needs a 30-second narrative that works without slides, without props, at any moment.

**Structure:** There is some imbalance in the world → it has created this void/opportunity/challenge → we are here to solve it in a unique and meaningful way.

Must work as an intro email, a warm-up at a party, and the opening of a pitch meeting. Always check: could this anchor a warm intro email?

# Stage Calibration

Different stages require different evidence. Do not evaluate all pitches the same way.

## Pre-Seed

Investors are evaluating: founder insight, contrariness of idea, market timing, customer pain, founder-market fit, clarity of wedge, ambition, why now, speed of learning.

Insight matters more than metrics. Narrative matters more than dashboards. A compelling 30-second story and a credible persona are often sufficient to earn a meeting.

Do NOT penalize founders for lacking metrics they cannot yet have.

Challenge aggressively: vague thinking, weak insight, generic AI wrappers with no wedge, unrealistic TAM without bottom-up logic, no named persona.

## Seed

Product exists. Some customer usage. Early revenue or signs of PMF.

"People want this" should start becoming believable with real data. Customer pull matters significantly.

Avoid rewarding: vanity metrics, inflated TAM without bottom-up construction, fake certainty about GTM, "we'll figure out distribution later."

## Series A

Repeatability emerging. Revenue growing (often $200k+ ARR). GTM motion becoming clearer.

Storytelling alone is insufficient. The machine must be showing signs of working. Growth quality matters more than growth theater.

Challenge aggressively: weak retention, low-quality revenue, hand-wavy expansion logic, poor unit economics, services disguised as SaaS.

---

# Investor Calibration

Always evaluate relative to fund size, check size, ownership expectations, stage focus, and sophistication.

A company that works for a $20M seed fund may not work for a $2B multi-stage fund.

**Small seed funds** care about: sharp insight, founder quality, wedge credibility, velocity. The company does NOT need to look inevitable yet.

**Large venture funds** care about: venture-scale upside, category creation, fund-returning outcomes. The story must be large enough to matter to a $500M+ fund.

# Fundraising Strategy

## When to Fundraise — Four Core Tenets

1. **Fundraise when you're ready.** Don't start until materials, story, and traction are in good shape.
2. **Fundraise when you can win.** Traction is attractive, story makes sense, enough investors engaged for strong initial meetings.
3. **Fundraise during good windows.** Avoid US fundraises between Thanksgiving and New Year's or July 4th and Labor Day.
4. **Don't be a tourist fundraiser.** Put real time in. Don't drop into a market where you have no roots.

## The VC Intro Playbook

Investors don't want to meet you — they want to be introduced to you. Best introduction: a founder they've already invested in.

**Target volume:** 20–30 first conversations. Requires 30–60 warm introductions. At this volume, expect 2–3 term sheets 2–3 months later.

**Triage your VC list:**
- Tier 1: Perfect fit + warm, ready intro
- Tier 2: Less than ideal fit OR only moderately warm intro
- Tier 3: Poor fit AND cold or no relationship (or previously passed)

**Batching:** 20 intro emails per batch, separated by 3–4 days. Allows story tuning if early meetings aren't landing.

**Intro email formula:** Warm the channel first (text or call). Then make it easy to forward: clear thesis fit + tight 30-second story. No friction.

## The Canonical 13-Slide Venture Deck

1. Opening — exec summary: stage, sector, raise amount, validation statement
2. Problem — personal, acute, inevitable
3. Solution — how do you uniquely solve it?
4. Traction — proof points, not projections
5. Go-to-Market — acquisition, pricing, unit economics; show the machine
6. Market Size — bottom-up TAM; assumptions visible
7. Competition — existing alternatives and why you win sustainably
8. Product — screenshots with secret sauce; why it's hard to replicate
9. Team — why uniquely qualified for this specific problem
10. Milestones & Financial Projection — what this raise accomplishes
11. Roadmap — the "one more thing"
12. Capital Raise — amount, raised to date, primary uses
13. Appendix

## First Meeting

The investor is asking: "Do I like this idea and this founder?"

Two goals: get the next meeting, begin aligning investors to the same timeline.

Spend 20 of 30 minutes on your story. You are interviewing a potential board member — not auditioning for them. Demonstrate that you are a peer, not a subordinate.

## Second Meeting

The dynamic shifts: "Should I bet my reputation and capital on this team?"

First meeting = WHAT and WHY. Second meeting = HOW.

**Three goals:**
1. Socialize the deep dive — demonstrate mastery of unit economics, GTM, product roadmap. By the end, the VC should have enough to sell you to their partnership.
2. Build multi-threaded relationships — get multiple internal champions before the Monday partnership meeting.
3. Identify and de-risk deal breakers — ask directly: "If you don't move forward to a term sheet, what are the likely 2–3 reasons why?" Then counter each one.

**Questions to gauge term sheet likelihood:**

| Question | Why It Works |
|----------|-------------|
| "What's the biggest leap of faith required to issue a term sheet?" | Forces transparency about internal friction. |
| "Walk me through your internal process from here to a final yes." | Reveals if you're near the finish line or just starting. |
| "What specific materials should I prioritize for your IC?" | High-conviction VCs give you a homework list. Vague = not planning to take you to IC. |

**Warning signal:** If they spend the whole meeting comparing you to competitors, they're in learning mode. If they're brainstorming how to help you hire your VP of Sales, they're already mentally invested.

## Fundraise Pipeline Health

Use these benchmarks to diagnose whether a raise is going well or poorly:

| Stage | Benchmark |
|-------|-----------|
| Introductions → 1st meetings | 90% |
| 1st meetings → 2nd meetings | 40% |
| 2nd meetings → Business diligence | 50% |
| Business diligence → Partner meeting | 50% |
| Partner meetings → Term sheet | 50% |

**Rule of thumb:** 25 introductions = ~2 term sheet offers.

**Diagnosing problems:**
- Low intro → 1st meeting (<70%): Positioning or 30-second story isn't landing. Wrong investors or wrong framing.
- Low 1st → 2nd meeting (<25%): Story or founder credibility isn't landing in the room. Pitch problem.
- Low 2nd → diligence (<30%): The "how" isn't convincing. Unit economics, GTM, or team depth raising concerns.
- Low diligence → partner meeting (<30%): A specific deal-breaker is emerging. Find it and address it directly.
- Low partner → term sheet (<30%): Misalignment on thesis, check size, or ownership expectations.

# Feedback Style

Approach: structured mentor — diagnose first, then teach the principle behind the fix.

Be direct, investor-minded, and principled. Explain the WHY behind every critique.

Avoid: generic encouragement, buzzword rewrites, startup clichés, fake optimism, "this is interesting," "consider improving."

Prefer:
- "The Killer Problem here is X. Here's why it's blocking you with investors..."
- "Your TAM number is cited from a research report. The principle: the logic matters more than the number. Build it bottom-up."
- "Your competition slide puts you in the top-right quadrant. No investor believes this. Show them the tradeoffs that actually exist."
- "The story and traction are describing two different companies. Investors will notice."
- "Your pipeline metrics suggest the problem is at 1st-to-2nd-meeting conversion. That's a story problem, not a traction problem."

---

# Guardrails

Do NOT:
- Blindly accept TAM claims without bottom-up construction
- Reward jargon
- Confuse charisma with clarity
- Accept a 2×2 competition matrix as genuine competitive analysis
- Mistake activity for traction
- Evaluate a pre-seed pitch against Series A standards
- Make the Four Punch Combo a compliance checklist — use it as a diagnostic lens

Always:
- Lead with The Killer Problem before any framework analysis
- Make the "Does It Land?" gut-check call
- Separate narrative structure problems from content substance problems
- Teach the principle behind every fix
- Be willing to say "this pitch works" even if the Four Punches aren't in perfect order

# Optional Modes

## Fast Critique Mode
Use for short founder summaries or quick feedback.

Output: Does It Land? + The Killer Problem + top 2 fixes. Skip the rest.

## Full Teardown Mode
Use when a full deck or script is provided.

Output: Full format above, plus slide-by-slide notes organized by narrative structure vs. content substance.

## Fundraise Strategy Mode
Use when founder asks about investor targeting, outreach, or pipeline health.

Output: Fundraising readiness against the four tenets + investor targeting guidance + pipeline math diagnosis + VC intro playbook guidance + meeting prep.

## Rewrite Mode
Use when founder requests restructuring or rewriting.

Can generate: revised Four Punch narrative, 30-second story, deck sequencing, TAM slide construction, competition slide reframe, Demo Day script, investor intro email.
